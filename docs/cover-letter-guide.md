# Cover letter guide: what actually works

Adapted from a guide by a writer at Kickresume who spent ten years helping people with resumes
and cover letters.

Most recruiters don't read cover letters anymore. But when one does matter, most fail because
they're either too generic or trying too hard to sound impressive. A good cover letter is just a
clear, honest conversation between you and the hiring manager. No gimmicks, no jargon.

## 1. Start with the job description

Most job posts are a mess of buzzwords. Cut through it:

- Focus on the "What you'll do" section. The top three tasks they list are their biggest pain
  points.
- Match your experience to those tasks, even if the match is modest. If the job says "manage
  email campaigns" and you've run a newsletter for your book club, write: "I've designed and
  sent monthly newsletters for a 200+ member group, growing engagement by 40%."

Don't over-index on the "requirements." If you meet 60-70% of the must-haves, apply. People land
roles missing two or three "requirements" all the time because they framed their experience
well. One job seeker got hired for a "5 years experience" role with just two years — her letter
showed she understood the job, even though her resume was light.

## 2. Match your skills to their needs (without forcing it)

Think of this as connecting dots, not reinventing yourself. Take a piece of paper and draw two
columns:

- Left: their needs (e.g. "Improve customer retention").
- Right: your relevant experience that speaks to each one.

Use their language. If the job says "CRM," say "CRM," not "customer database." This isn't about
tricking anyone; it's about speaking their dialect.

Stuck? Ask: "What's the core problem this job solves?" If it's a customer service role, they
probably want someone who can calm upset clients — so highlight a time you did exactly that.

## 3. Research the company

Hiring managers want to know you care about the company, not just the paycheck. It's a little
hypocritical, but they make the rules, so play along. Ten minutes is enough:

- Check the "About Us" page. Do they value innovation? Community? Mention it.
- Skim their blog or LinkedIn for recent work. For example: "I saw your team launched a
  mentorship program last month — I've mentored interns at my current job and loved it."

Avoid generic fluff. Find something real.

- Weak: "Your company is a leader in the industry."
- Better: "I admire how your company partners with local schools for STEM workshops. As a former
  teacher, I'd love to contribute to that mission."

Can't find anything? Fall back to the job itself: "I'm drawn to this role because I thrive in
collaborative environments, and the team's focus on X lines up with my experience in Y."

## 4. Write the letter

A straightforward template — the more you customize it, the better:

```
Hi [Hiring Manager's Name],

I'm [Your Name], a [Your Title/Field] with experience in [Key Skill 1] and [Key Skill 2].
When I saw your opening for [Job Title], I knew my background in [Relevant Experience] could
help [Company] [solve a problem they mentioned].

At [Current/Last Job], I [Achievement 1]. For example, [specific story with numbers or
outcomes]. I also [Achievement 2], where I [brief example].

What excites me most about [Company] is [specific value/project/initiative]. [Optional:
personal connection.] I'd love to bring my [skill/passion] to your team and help [impact you
want to make].

I'd appreciate the chance to discuss how I can contribute to [Company]. You can reach me at
[Phone] or [Email]. Thank you for your time — I look forward to hearing from you.

Best regards,
[Your Name]
```

You can use an AI cover letter generator (Kickresume, Zety, Novoresume) for a first draft, and
they work fine. But always heavily edit the result — you want it to sound like you, not like a
machine.

## Common mistakes

- **Repeating your resume.** Focus on context, not a list of jobs.
- **Forgetting the "why."** Always link your skills to their needs. Done right: "Your job
  description mentions streamlining workflows — a challenge I tackled at my last role by
  implementing a project management tool that cut meeting time by 20%."

## If you're underqualified

Be honest, but focus on growth: "While I'm newer to [skill], I've spent the last [X months]
building it through [course/project/volunteering]. For example, [specific achievement]. I'm
eager to keep learning in a role like this."

A real example from a career-changer with no formal HR experience: "I've spent the last year
volunteering as a hiring coordinator for a nonprofit, where I screened 100+ applicants and
improved our onboarding process. I'm ready to bring that hands-on experience to a full-time
role."

## Final tip: read it aloud

If it sounds stiff or robotic, rewrite it in your natural voice. Imagine you're explaining to a
friend why you're a good fit.

## TL;DR

Connect your experience to their problems. Clarity beats cleverness every time — keep it simple,
specific, and human.
